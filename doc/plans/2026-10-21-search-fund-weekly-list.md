# Search-fund weekly list

Date: 2026-10-21

Status: concept note for a possible service. This is not a Paperclip product change.

## Concept

A new searcher pays a subscription and gets a Paperclip company that already does one job. Once a week it rebuilds their company list from public sources, under their buy box and their exclusion list. They edit those two inputs. They receive a file of companies. They do not hire agents, pick adapters, or learn the control plane.

The data is public: association directories, maps, state business filings, and the company website. There is no proprietary database to build. The customization is the buy box and the exclusion list.

Two agents do the work. One gathers the rows. One writes each row as "pass," with the public fact that removed it, or "keep," with the question only the owner can answer. Paperclip schedules the run, caps the spend, and stops for a human to check a sample of the links before the file goes out. The service does not email owners and does not take a fee if a company is later bought.

The same run is the marketing. One published buy box produces one public sample list. A searcher who wants that list for their own buy box subscribes. The sample is the pitch. The agents do not recruit.

The subscription has to sit on the tools line these searchers already publish, about $2,000 to $10,000 a year. That is roughly $170 to $400 a month, or one paid list in the first month and the same price if they keep it. A $1,500 to $2,500 monthly retainer is a salary decision, and this concept does not ask for that.

Paperclip is the engine under that subscription. The searcher buys the weekly file of companies.

## Buyer

The buyer is one investor-backed searcher in the first months of a search, before an intern is doing the list.

A traditional searcher raises money from a small group of investors, then spends the search looking for one company to buy and run. Stanford's 2026 study says the 2024–25 U.S. and Canadian cohort raised a median of $550,000 per principal and $600,000 per fund, from a median of 13 investors, in about three months. Among companies bought since 2020, the median search lasted 20 months.

Salary in that budget is commonly described at $100,000 to $150,000 a year. One term-sheet guide puts monthly burn at $15,000 to $25,000. Permitted uses include salary, travel, legal, and data tools.

A self-funded searcher, working from roughly $100,000 to $150,000 of their own money, is a poor fit. A private-equity fund is also the wrong buyer. A fund already has associates.

Practitioner budgets put the whole outreach-and-software line at about $2,000 to $10,000 a year. A retainer of $1,500 to $2,500 a month would be a salary-sized decision, not a software purchase. The subscription in the concept section stays inside that yearly tools line. The first charge can be a single list. The renewal is the same weekly file.

## Market

Stanford counted 181 new U.S. and Canadian core search funds across 2024 and 2025. The figure 862 is every known core fund since 1984, including people who already bought, already quit, or already sold. Stanford does not publish how many of those funds are searching in a given month. With a 20-month median search, the people who can write the check are that recent cohort plus whoever from the prior cohort is still looking. The near-term business is one or two paying searchers.

Axial added 2,635 new buyers in 2025, up 36 percent. Those buyers are every buyer type, not searchers. Search funds were 14 percent of Axial's closed deals that year, at an average value of $11.9 million. That is a mix of closed deals, not a count of customers.

Two substitutes already cover the obvious versions of the job:

- Interns build the list. A 2026 Searchfunder posting for a half-time sourcing intern drew 244 applicants. The usual arrangement is 15 to 20 hours a week, and many of these internships are unpaid.
- Coseek sells research plus calling. Its published four-week sprint is $4,500 and includes list building, enrichment, and 20 calling days.

The open seat is a new searcher who does not yet have an intern and does not want a calling agency.

## Workflow

Stanford's 2014 search-phase guide, which operators still cite, says about 80 percent of the week is proprietary search and about 20 percent is brokers and admin. The slow step is the list: company names, what they do, the owner's name, and a way to contact them. The same guide says not to analyze a company before knowing whether the owner might sell, and to spend 5 to 15 minutes getting ready for a call.

Public pages can support that list. They cannot support a financial memo. A website can show the service, the cities, the locations, and sometimes the founder's name. It cannot show revenue, EBITDA, or willingness to sell. Only the owner can confirm those.

One paid week:

1. The searcher sends a buy box and a do-not-contact list. The buy box is one industry, one region, a size clue such as locations or employees, and the exclusions.
2. An agent builds rows from directories, maps, state filings, and the company site. Each row has the company, the city, a short quote of what the company says it does, the source link, the owner's name only if the site prints it, and either "pass" with the public fact that removed it or "keep" with the question only the owner can answer. No revenue figure is invented.
3. A person opens a sample of the links before the file goes out.
4. The searcher makes the calls. The service does not email owners and does not take a fee if the searcher later buys a company.

A second paid week is the same file with the new exclusions added. That repeat is where Paperclip fits: a weekly run, a human approves the batch, and a budget cap stops the run. Owner contact stays off the board.

## Legal boundary

Identifying sellers for a fee tied to a closed sale is a different business. Law-firm summaries treat transaction-based pay, and identifying buyers or sellers in a sale, as factors that can pull the work toward broker registration. A flat fee for a research file the searcher uses alone is the shape this note describes. A lawyer should read that offer before the first invoice. This note is not legal advice.

## Assumptions that failed

- The buyer pool is not Axial's 2,635 new buyers, and it is not the historical 862 funds. It is the recent searching cohort.
- A multi-thousand-dollar monthly retainer does not fit the published tools budget of a few thousand dollars a year.
- A long memo with revenue and EBITDA is the wrong deliverable. Those figures are not public, and the search guide says not to write that memo before the owner might sell.
- The job is not empty. Interns do it for little or no pay, and a calling firm sells research plus calls for $4,500 over four weeks.

## Assumption that held

The slow work is real: build a sourced list, mark public passes, and leave owner-only questions open. A new searcher without an intern can subscribe for that list at a tools-budget price. The public sample list is how the offer sells. Paperclip repeats the list after they subscribe. It does not create the first sale by recruiting on its own.
